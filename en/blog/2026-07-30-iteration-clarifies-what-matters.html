1. Looking for one answer

For a long time, I thought life might yield one right answer: the right career, the right city, the right philosophy. The search made thinking feel productive, but it did not make the next move much clearer.

What changed was not finding a better answer. It was returning to the world. Trying, noticing what happened, and trying again became more useful than searching for a final answer. The pattern appears in work, writing, health, money, and relationships. They look unlike one another until the same question surfaces in each: what do I learn by doing this again, with a little more attention?

Trying something gives an assumption a chance to survive or disappear. A new role, a piece of writing, a trip, or a conversation can answer in ways that planning alone cannot.

This is a working theory of life, version 0.1. Iteration can clarify, test, and sometimes reshape what matters; it does not prove that meaning was fully formed in advance.

2. Returning to reality

Writing and yoga are not finished. Each asks for another encounter with what is actually happening: attention, adjustment, and the humility to begin again. That ongoing willingness is what I mean by Practice.

Within it, an iteration is one replaceable attempt. A plan cannot answer back. Reality can.

I think of Practice as a simple return: make an attempt, meet reality, and come back with a clearer next move.

Solid arrows show the continuing practice. Dotted arrows show what an encounter with reality may reveal or leave behind.

Not every return produces clarity, and not every attempt leaves something useful behind. The dotted lines are possibilities, not promises. What matters is staying answerable to reality.

Clarity can confirm a direction, expose a weak assumption, or make a once-important aim feel less true. The point is not to complete a framework. It is to stay in contact with a life as it is lived.

3. Making room

Some complexity is essential: care, responsibilities, and the ordinary work of keeping a life going. Some is chosen because it is worth the cost. Not every burden is noise; some complexity is the shape of commitment.

Other complexity is accidental or inertial: obligations nobody has reconsidered, maintenance that no longer serves a purpose, possessions, and decisions that fill the day before anything important can enter it. When life becomes crowded, even a useful answer can be hard to hear.

Reducing the unnecessary part creates capacity: time, attention, energy, money, emotional space, and optionality. It also creates safety—the room to take a meaningful risk and to decline a meaningless one. Capacity creates choice, not an obligation to optimize or produce. It can be used for exploration, creation, care, play, recovery, family, or doing nothing. The room need not be filled.

The point is not to make life constant optimization. It is to make enough room for honest feedback. Reality has more texture than a plan; it answers when an attempt gives it something to answer.

4. What remains

Not every iteration succeeds. A project may end, a plan may change, or an assumption may fail. Yet an attempt may leave learning, a capability, or the support of someone who saw the effort. The result can matter even when the original plan does not.

Those things can support a later return, but they are not a scorecard. Some experiences build capacity; others are what capacity was for. Health, trust, and relationships are not assets to be accumulated. They are parts of a life that can be cared for, damaged, restored, and enjoyed.

Money fits here too: not as the objective, but as one source of optionality and safety. Its value is the freedom it may create to choose with more care.

5. What matters and what we carry

What matters belongs to a particular life, but it is rarely formed alone. For one person it may be family; for another, how they want to use their time. It can also take shape through friends, care, work, communities, responsibilities, and meaning. Clarity is not private proof. It can show us where care is needed, who is affected, and which purposes deserve to be carried.

Family does not fit under productivity. Time does not either: urgency, patience, and long-term compounding are questions of attention before they are questions of efficiency.

Commit deeply to what matters. Keep the means replaceable. A commitment may be worth its cost, while a particular routine, plan, role, or tool may not be the only way to honor it. That distinction makes room for change without treating care as disposable.

The theory should remain answerable to experience. Its names may change as life does; the test is whether it helps us notice, choose, and care more honestly.

6. Return

The next move does not need to settle a whole life. It can be small: make a little room, try something real, notice what happens, and return with more care for what the attempt has clarified.

Iteration does not create what matters. It clarifies it.
